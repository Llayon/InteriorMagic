## Source A: Wikipedia, "Feng shui" (en)

URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feng_shui
Retrieved 2026-08-23 via the MediaWiki "extracts" API.
Used for: school taxonomy (Form / Compass / BTB / Symbolic / New Age), historical
context, criticism context.

Key passages used directly:

- "Feng shui holds that one's external environment can affect one's internal
  state. A goal of the practice is to achieve a 'perfect spot', a location and an
  axis in time that can help one achieve a state of shū fú (舒服) or harmony with
  the universe."

- "Traditional feng shui is inherently a form of ancestor worship... was built
  on the idea that the ghosts of ancestors and other independent, intangible
  forces, both personal and impersonal, affected the material world, and that
  these forces needed to be placated through rites and suitable burial places."

- "The Form branch was originally concerned with the location and orientation
  of tombs (Yin House feng shui)... The branch then progressed to the
  consideration of homes and other buildings (Yang House feng shui)."

- "Compass branch is a collection of more recent feng shui techniques based on
  the Eight Directions, each of which is said to have unique qi. It uses the
  luopan."

- "BTB Black (Hat) Tantric Buddhist Sect (Westernised or modern methods not
  based on Classical teachings)" — explicitly categorized as a non-classical
  modern school.

- "Symbolic Feng Shui, (New Age feng shui methods that advocate substitution
  with symbolic (spiritual, appropriate representation of five elements) objects
  if natural environment or object/s is/are not available or viable)"

- Practitioner-side criticism cited from a Western scholar: "feng shui tends to
  be reduced to interior design for health and wealth. It has become
  increasingly visible through 'feng shui consultants' and corporate architects
  who charge large sums of money for their analysis, advice and design."

- Wikipedia's own editorial position: "In Western philosophy of science, feng
  shui is generally regarded as non-scientific, while some scientific skeptics
  have more narrowly classified it as a pseudoscience."

- Hong Kong Disneyland example (also in dedicated Wikipedia article): see
  Wikipedia, "Hong Kong Disneyland".

## Source B: Wikipedia, "Hong Kong Disneyland"

URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Disneyland
Used as a real-world example of how a multi-billion-dollar commercial
construction site explicitly incorporated feng shui consultation in 2005.

Direct excerpt:
"Notably, a bend was put in a walkway near the Hong Kong Disneyland Resort
entrance so good qi energy would not flow into the South China Sea."

This is useful as a documented practitioner-engineering intersection — not as a
basis for any specific Bedroom / Desk / Sofa rule.

## Source C: WoFS (World of Feng Shui, Lillian Too organization)

URL: https://www.wofs.com/landscape-feng-shui/designing-the-feng-shui-of-bedrooms/
Article: "Designing the Feng Shui of Bedrooms" by Chris Yeo, hosted on WoFS.com.
Retrieved 2026-08-23.

Used as a credible modern popular-practitioner source (Lillian Too is a
practitioner with 200+ books in 30+ languages, an MBA from Harvard Business
School, former banker and CEO; documented on Wikipedia). The article does not
itself contain the "command position" or "headboard against a solid wall"
sentences directly, but does discuss:
- balancing yin/yang via colour and lighting;
- mirror taboo in bedroom;
- avoiding under-mattress storage;
- wood-based design for romance sector.

This source is useful for confirming that mainstream Feng Shui practitioner
publishing engages seriously with interior-design-driven recommendations. It is
**not** by itself sufficient for any specific spatial rule.

## Source F: Wikipedia, "Bagua" and "Qi"

URLs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bagua and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qi

Used for the foundational concepts: bagua is a "set of symbols from China
intended to illustrate the nature of reality as comprising mutually opposing
forces reinforcing one another... has correspondences in astronomy, divination,
geography, geomancy (feng shui)". Qi page confirms qi is a "prescientific...
pseudoscientific concept, i.e. not corresponding to the concept of energy as
used in the physical sciences."

## Source G: Wikipedia references of scholarly provenance

From Wikipedia, "Feng shui" References:
- Bruun, Ole — Fengshui in China (NIAS Press, 2011) and An Introduction to
  Feng Shui (Cambridge, 2008). Academic anthropology / East Asian studies.
- Skinner, Stephen — Guide to the Feng Shui Compass (Golden Hoard, 2008).
  English-language non-academic but serious practitioner / historian.
- Paton, Michael John — Five Classics of Fengshui (Brill, 2013). Academic
  publisher.
- Allan, Sarah — Shape of the Turtle (SUNY Press, 1991). Major sinologist.
- Magli, Giulio — Sacred Landscapes of Imperial China (Springer, 2020).
  Archaeoastronomy.
- Vyse, Stuart — Superstition: A Very Short Introduction (OUP, 2020).
  Skeptical academic psychology / behavioral science.

All retrievable via standard scholarly book channels (the Wikipedia main
article cites these; many have public archive.org previews).
